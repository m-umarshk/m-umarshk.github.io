# How I Learned About Machine Learning & Deep Learning

<img src="/assets/images/machine-learning.jpeg"
alt="Machine Learning and Deep Learning"
style="width:100%; border-radius:12px; margin-bottom:20px;">

Before my first semester at university, the words "Machine Learning" and "Deep Learning" sounded like something out of a science fiction movie. I had heard these terms before, but I had no real understanding of what they meant or how they actually worked. All of that changed when Dr. Bilal Ahmad introduced us to these concepts during our semester, and what followed was one of the most eye-opening learning experiences I have had so far as a Computer Engineering student.

The way Dr. Bilal Ahmad explained Machine Learning made it feel less like a mysterious technology and more like a logical process that anyone could understand with the right foundation. He described it as teaching a computer to learn from data rather than programming it with fixed rules. Instead of writing instructions for every possible situation, you give the model examples and let it figure out the patterns on its own. That idea alone completely changed the way I thought about programming and what computers are truly capable of doing.

One of the first things we learned was how to train a machine learning model. Dr. Bilal walked us through the entire process — from collecting and preparing data, to choosing the right algorithm, to evaluating how well the model performed. Understanding this pipeline gave me a clear picture of how real-world machine learning projects are structured. It was not just about writing code. It was about thinking carefully about the problem, understanding the data, and making informed decisions at every step of the process.

We also learned about specific algorithms such as Logistic Regression and Random Forest. Logistic Regression was explained as a method for predicting outcomes that fall into categories — for example, whether something is true or false, yes or no, healthy or at risk. Random Forest, on the other hand, was described as a more powerful technique that builds many decision trees and combines their results to make more accurate predictions. Learning about these algorithms helped me understand that machine learning is not a single tool but a collection of different approaches, each suited for different kinds of problems.

What made everything truly meaningful was the project we worked on — predicting heart disease. Using a real medical dataset, we built a model that could analyze a person's health information and predict whether they were at risk of developing heart disease. The moment I saw the model making predictions, something clicked for me. This was not just an academic exercise. This was a tool that could genuinely help people. By identifying who might be at risk, doctors and healthcare workers could intervene earlier and potentially save lives. That realization gave the entire project a sense of purpose that went far beyond passing an exam.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad has always emphasized the importance of using medical datasets for machine learning projects, and after working on the heart disease prediction project, I completely understood why. Medical data is collected with a high level of precision and accuracy. Every data point represents a real person's health information, which means the stakes are high and the quality of the data matters enormously. Working with this kind of dataset taught me to take data seriously, to clean it carefully, and to think about what the numbers actually represent in the real world. You can explore Dr. Bilal Ahmad's research work and published papers on his [Google Scholar profile](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en).

Beyond the technical skills, this experience taught me something deeper about the purpose of machine learning. Technology is most powerful when it is used to solve problems that matter to people. Heart disease is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and the idea that a well-trained model could help predict it and assist in early diagnosis was genuinely motivating. It made me think differently about my role as a future engineer — not just as someone who writes code, but as someone who can build tools that make a real difference in people's lives.

Deep Learning was also introduced to us during this period, and while we did not go into extreme depth, the foundation that Dr. Bilal Ahmad laid was enough to spark genuine curiosity. Understanding that Deep Learning involves neural networks inspired by the human brain, and that it powers everything from image recognition to language translation, made me eager to learn more in the semesters ahead. The connection between what I was learning and the technologies I use every day made the subject feel alive and relevant.

Looking back, learning about Machine Learning and Deep Learning during my first semester was one of the most valuable experiences of my academic journey so far. It expanded my understanding of what computers can do, gave me practical experience with real data, and showed me how technology can be directed toward meaningful goals. Thanks to the guidance of Dr. Bilal Ahmad, I left that semester not just with new technical knowledge, but with a clearer sense of what kind of engineer I want to become. The heart disease prediction project, in particular, reminded me that behind every dataset there are real people — and that is a responsibility worth taking seriously.
