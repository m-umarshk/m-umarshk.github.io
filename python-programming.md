# My First Experience with Python Programming

The moment I first opened a Python file on my screen, I honestly had no idea what I was looking at. Lines of text, strange symbols, and words I had never seen before filled the editor. Everything felt foreign, and for a brief moment I wondered whether I had made the right choice by joining Computer Engineering. That feeling of uncertainty, however, turned out to be just the beginning of one of the most rewarding journeys of my first semester.

In those early weeks, Professor Dr. Bilal Ahmad made it clear that understanding the syntax of Python was the foundation of everything else. Rather than jumping straight into complex programs, he focused on teaching us how to read and write code correctly, how Python expects you to structure your statements, and why small mistakes like missing a colon or wrong indentation can break everything. This approach was different from what I had imagined. I expected to be writing big programs, but instead I was learning how to write a single correct line, and that made all the difference. Dr. Bilal's method of teaching syntax first gave me a mental framework that I could build on as the semester progressed.

My very first program was the classic "Hello, World!" — a single line that prints a message on the screen. When I ran it and saw those two words appear, I felt a small but real sense of accomplishment. It sounds simple, but that moment confirmed to me that I could make a computer do something by writing just one line. From there, I moved on to variables, data types, and basic input and output operations. Every new concept added another piece to the puzzle, and slowly the code started to look less like a foreign language and more like something I could understand.

One area where I genuinely struggled was loops. Whether it was a `for` loop going through a list or a `while` loop running based on a condition, I found it difficult to visualize what the program was doing at each step. I would write the loop, run the code, and then stare at the output trying to figure out what had happened. It took me several tries and a lot of practice before I began to understand how loops work from the inside. I remember spending extra time going over examples, running small test programs, and tracing through the logic step by step. Loops eventually became one of my strongest areas, but only because I refused to skip past the confusion.

Throughout this process, the YouTube lectures by Dr. Bilal Ahmad proved to be an incredibly valuable resource. Whenever a concept felt unclear after class, I would revisit it through his videos, where he would walk through examples in a calm and structured way. His explanations had a quality that made difficult ideas feel approachable. He did not just show what to write — he explained why, and that understanding of the reasoning behind the syntax helped me retain what I was learning far better than memorization ever could. I found myself watching certain videos more than once just to make sure I had fully grasped the concept.

You can explore Dr. Bilal Ahmad's Python programming lectures on his YouTube channel: [Dr. Bilal on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@DrBilal). For those interested in his academic research and published work, his profile is also available on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en).

As the weeks passed, something began to shift. Concepts that had once confused me started to feel natural. I was no longer staring at the screen wondering where to begin. I would open a problem, think about it for a moment, and start writing. The logic of programming — breaking a big problem into smaller steps, using variables to store information, and using loops to repeat actions — began to feel like a way of thinking rather than just a set of rules. That transition, from confusion to confidence, is something I will always remember about my first semester.

Looking back, my first experience with Python was not smooth or effortless. It was full of small frustrations, repeated mistakes, and moments where I genuinely did not understand what was going wrong. But each of those moments taught me something. I learned to slow down, to read error messages carefully, to test my code in small pieces, and to keep going even when progress felt slow. The guidance of Dr. Bilal Ahmad, both in the classroom and through his online content, played a major role in helping me build that foundation. Python became the starting point of my journey as a Computer Engineering student, and the habits I developed in that course have continued to shape the way I approach every new challenge since.
