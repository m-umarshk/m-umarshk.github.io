# Chronic Kidney Disease Prediction – My DBMS and ML Project

<img src="/assets/images/ckd-prediction.jpeg"
alt="Chronic Kidney Disease Prediction"
style="width:100%; border-radius:12px; margin-bottom:20px;">

One of the most meaningful projects I completed during my second semester was the Chronic Kidney Disease Prediction system — a project that combined both Database Management Systems and Machine Learning into a single, complete application. The goal was to build a system where a patient could enter their medical information and receive a prediction about whether they were at risk of chronic kidney disease. Building this project on my own from start to finish was one of the most challenging and rewarding things I did throughout my entire second semester.

The idea behind the project came directly from the approach that Dr. Bilal Ahmad had introduced us to during our first semester — using machine learning on real medical datasets to solve problems that have genuine value in the real world. Chronic kidney disease is a serious condition that affects millions of people worldwide, and early detection can make a significant difference in treatment outcomes. Using technology to help identify who might be at risk felt like exactly the kind of purpose-driven work that Dr. Bilal had always encouraged us to pursue. His philosophy of directing machine learning toward problems that truly matter had stayed with me, and this project was my opportunity to apply it independently.

The first step was training the machine learning model. I used two algorithms — Decision Tree and Logistic Regression — and compared their performance on the kidney disease dataset. The dataset contained real medical records with features such as age, blood pressure, specific gravity, albumin levels, sugar levels, blood glucose, blood urea, serum creatinine, sodium, and potassium levels. Each of these values represents a real measurement from a patient's medical record, which meant that working with this data required care and precision. After training and evaluating both models, the final system achieved an accuracy of 98%, which was an incredibly satisfying result and confirmed that the model was genuinely capable of making reliable predictions.

Once the model was trained and performing well, I moved on to building the graphical user interface. The GUI was designed to be clean and easy to use — a patient or healthcare worker could open the application, enter the relevant medical values into clearly labeled input fields, and receive a prediction about kidney disease risk. The fields included age, blood pressure, specific gravity, albumin, sugar, blood glucose random, blood urea, serum creatinine, sodium, and potassium, each with their respective units clearly displayed. Making the interface intuitive and professional was important to me because the whole point of the project was that real people should be able to use it without any technical knowledge.

The database component of the project tied everything together. Patient records entered through the GUI were stored and managed using proper database design principles — the same concepts I had been learning throughout the semester in Database Management Systems. Designing the database structure, setting up the tables, and ensuring that the data flowed correctly between the interface, the database, and the machine learning model required me to apply knowledge from multiple subjects simultaneously. It was the first time I had truly integrated everything I had learned into one complete system, and the experience of making it all work together was unlike anything I had done before.

Building this entire project on my own pushed me to think independently and take full responsibility for every decision. There was no teammate to share the load or consult when something went wrong. Every bug, every design choice, and every technical challenge was mine to solve. It was demanding, but it also gave me a deep sense of ownership over the final result. When the system was complete and running — when I could enter a patient's medical data and see the model return a prediction with 98% accuracy — I felt genuinely proud of what I had built.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad's influence was present throughout this project, even though it was completed independently. The foundation he had given us in machine learning during our first semester — understanding how to train models, work with medical data, and think about real-world impact — was the backbone of everything I built. His research focus on using machine learning to solve meaningful problems had shaped the way I approached this project from the very beginning. For anyone interested in his work in AI, ML, and data-driven research, his profile is available on [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en) and [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/).

The Chronic Kidney Disease Prediction project was more than just an assignment. It was proof that the knowledge I had been building over two semesters could be combined into something real, useful, and meaningful. It reminded me of why I chose Computer Engineering — not just to write code, but to build things that can genuinely help people.
